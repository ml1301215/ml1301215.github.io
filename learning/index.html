    Learning

    Learning Journey

    My learning trajectory falls roughly into two stages. The problems and goals of each stage differ, but the methodology is consistent: build the framework first, then do the precise work.

    Stage 1 (6 Years) — Mathematics, and More Precisely, Algebraic Geometry

    My introduction to mathematics came from a course at USTC called Abstract Algebra.

    Algebra struck me as very elegant, especially the Frobenius change of basis.

    Unlike other math courses, this one did not follow a fixed textbook. It started from the most basic concepts and derived things in a free-ranging way. The instructor occasionally got stuck, but when that happened, he would think for a moment and then continue using an approach of his own — possibly different from the textbook. (Those moments were often when you learned the most.)

    The most remarkable thing about the course was that I found I could do the textbook exercises directly after attending lectures.

    The textbook was Professor Ouyang Yi's Abstract Algebra.

    I was fortunate that the instructor of that course later became my master's thesis advisor.

    My path through algebraic geometry was:
    Abstract Algebra → Category Theory → Commutative Algebra → Introduction to Algebraic Geometry → Advanced Algebraic Geometry → Algebraic Surfaces → Bogomolov-Type Inequality on Quasi-Elliptic Surfaces (thesis topic)

    My graduate research focused on algebraic geometry in positive characteristic. For the relevant technical background, see Notes on Algebraic Geometry in Positive Characteristic.

    Stage 2: AI — More Precisely, AI Technology, and Everything That Helped Me Understand It (January 2025 to Present)

    Before 2025, I did not think AI was capable of handling mathematical problems or reasoning the way a person does.

    That changed completely when DeepSeek-R1 was released in January 2025.

    It seemed to actually think. I was genuinely surprised.

    By then the direction for my thesis was already clear. So I went and learned everything I could about AI.

    My approach was different from a traditional computer science student's. I did not grind LeetCode, and I did not spend large amounts of time watching CS or AI lectures.

    My only prior computing background was Python, C++, and data structures (undergraduate general education courses), but I wanted to learn more, so I did.

    My learning philosophy: use AI to learn about AI; learn through practice and real projects.

    I started by building a few WeChat mini-games with AI assistance.

    In the process, I learned Python syntax alongside AI-assisted development until I could understand the overall structure of AI-generated code — how a frontend is organized, what backend code does, how the two connect, and so on.

    I also picked up the basics of commonly used tools, like Docker and Git.

    A few lessons from this experience worth sharing:

    1. The most important skill when using AI as a tool is knowing how to communicate with it.
    Note: AI output is fundamentally a probability distribution. Even given identical inputs and even at temperature=0, outputs are not deterministic (see this blog post for a discussion of that topic). For critical business scenarios, do not rely on exact string matching — design more robust system logic that focuses on semantic correctness rather than literal consistency. If you need to extract structured information from LLM output, use regular expressions or a dedicated parser rather than expecting a perfectly consistent format every time. If you are working with open-source models, consider using batch-variant operator libraries to enforce independence across batch elements.

    2. My job when working with AI is to verify that the AI's output meets my requirements, then provide thorough feedback based on that. This includes reading and understanding what the AI produces — code implementations, code structure, demonstrations, bug fixes — as well as judging the work itself: does it match my expectations, is it using the best approach, could it be done better?

    3. Many failures are not caused by AI limitations, but by:

    1. A problem description that is not sufficiently clear and precise.
    2. Insufficient verification and testing of the AI's output.
    3. Failing to use an AI system better suited to the specific problem at hand.

    With this experience and knowledge, I passed the internship interview at JD.com.

    During my internship at JD.com (summer 2025), I worked on more substantial projects within the team, including RAG and multi-agent systems.

    At that time, AI had not yet entered actual production projects at most companies, and the AI exploration efforts of many large tech firms were just beginning.

    JD.com was in the same situation, attempting its own AI transformation.

    The project I worked on was called "Multi-Agent Automated Vulnerability Discovery." Its goal was to rapidly develop, within one to two months, a complete automated system exploring the possibility of using AI for automated penetration testing and vulnerability discovery.

    During the development, I learned a great deal.

    For example: how to use AI coding tools effectively — tools like Trae, Cursor, and Claude Code.
    How to find genuinely high-quality information (rather than relying entirely on AI) — arXiv, GitHub, and so on.
    How to use AI to quickly read and understand research papers, and present the findings via slides or Dingtalk documents.
    How RAG retrieval should be implemented.
    How to use AI development platforms like Dify and Coze.

    After this project ended, due to an organizational change in my team's leadership at JD.com, I left my JD.com internship and, after three rounds of interviews, joined Chaitin Tech for another internship.

    My work at Chaitin Tech focused mainly on fine-tuning and reinforcement learning training for large language models. Over two months there, I completed the full pipeline for a model training project: a 2-stage fine-tuning process to train a large model for security alert analysis.

    For a company in the network security space, helping customers identify genuine threats quickly and accurately is critical. Alert analysis can be understood as: the process of finding real threats among a massive volume of fragmented security alerts and determining their severity.

    The traditional approach to network alert analysis is manual analysis with human labeling. AI is believed to greatly improve the efficiency of this process and automate it.

    The difficulty of using a general-purpose large model for alert analysis is that judging the severity of an alert from its raw information requires specialized security knowledge that open-source base models do not possess. Fine-tuned and reinforcement-trained models can acquire this knowledge, and security companies have extensive labeled data available for model training. That is what motivated this project.

    After completing the full 2-stage training, I also evaluated the model on the company's internal benchmark dataset.

    I am glad to report that the final trained model achieved alert prediction accuracy of 98+%, an improvement of nearly 60 percentage points over the pre-training baseline. For the technical details, see LLM Fine-Tuning Notes.

    During the training process I encountered many problems, and also found a number of methods for working more efficiently. I have shared those lessons below (each one represents a blood-and-sweat lesson — some cost me several hours, others a whole day of overtime):

    • How to set up an environment for reinforcement learning and fine-tuning training on a brand-new server?
    • What should you watch out for when using Docker?
    • Which Linux server tools can greatly improve your productivity and experience?

    Later, in January 2026, I passed two written tests and one interview and joined the Zhongguancun Academy of Artificial Intelligence to pursue a deeper exploration of the boundaries of AI.

    The first direction was to explore the mathematical boundaries of AI, drawing on my own background.

    I open-sourced a project called Vibe-Proving, which has problem-solving, learning, theorem retrieval, LaTeX code generation, and auto-formalization features — all designed to use the most advanced AI tools to assist mathematical research. In the process, I used Vibe-Proving to attempt problems from my own research area (some open, some with known results): a series of Chomp-related problems, exercises from GTM52, and a conjecture posed by a mathematics professor at USTC.

    Unfortunately, AI did not make any substantive mathematical contribution in this process. But from AI's failures I gained a clearer picture of where its boundaries lie. Here are a few patterns of failure:

    1. Hallucination: theorem statements cited from papers do not match the original propositions; AI will distort a theorem's description in a paper in order to get the result it wants.
    2. AI is not slow enough: mathematical research is a multi-dimensional, complex, and highly iterative process. Although a finished paper is built almost entirely on rigorous, polished proofs, a mathematician's day-to-day work has long been understood to involve activities that are traditionally not made public. Behind the final formal account lies a deep exploration: initial intuitions are tested, counterexamples are found, and core definitions and proofs go through repeated cycles of conjecture and refutation.

    The second direction was to explore the memory boundaries of AI. I helped integrate multimodal capabilities into LATRACE-AI (project coverage: OmniMemory). The technical design of the full-modal pipeline is documented in this blog post.
