    Notes on Algebraic Geometry in Positive Characteristic

    ← Learning

    Compiled from background and proof techniques accumulated while studying the Bogomolov-type inequality on quasi-elliptic surfaces; the result is on the publication page. Aimed at readers with a working knowledge of algebraic geometry (schemes, cohomology).

    Introduction: Positive Characteristic as a Testing Ground

    A recurring question in algebraic geometry is whether classical theorems still hold over fields of positive characteristic. Sometimes the answer is yes, but the proof has to take a different route. Sometimes the answer is no, and the counterexample reveals geometric structure that was hidden in the characteristic-0 case.

    The Bogomolov inequality is in the first category. It has a clean proof in characteristic 0, but extending it to positive characteristic requires understanding how Frobenius interacts with vector-bundle stability. When the surface carries a quasi-elliptic fibration, that interaction becomes more delicate — quasi-elliptic fibrations exist only in characteristics 2 and 3.

    These notes start from vector-bundle basics and build up the tools needed to engage with this problem: slope stability, the Bogomolov inequality, the Frobenius morphism, quasi-elliptic fibrations, and the points where the classical proof breaks down.

    Preliminary I: Vector Bundles and Stability

    Basic Invariants of a Vector Bundle

    Let S be a smooth projective surface over an algebraically closed field k, and let E be a rank-r vector bundle on S. Its main numerical invariants are the Chern classes:

    • c1(E) ∈ Pic(S) — the first Chern class, corresponding to the determinant line bundle det(E) = ∧rE
    • c2(E) ∈ H4(S, ℤ) ≅ ℤ — the second Chern class
    • discriminant Δ(E) = 2r · c2(E) − (r−1) · c1(E)2

    For End(E), one has c2(End(E)) = 2r · c2(E) − (r−1) · c1(E)2 = Δ(E), so the discriminant equals the second Chern class of the endomorphism bundle.

    Slope Stability (Mumford–Takemoto)

    Fix an ample divisor H on S (a polarization). The slope of E with respect to H is

    μ(E) = (c1(E) · H) / rank(E)

    where c1(E) · H is the intersection number.

    Definition (slope stability): a vector bundle E is H-semistable if for every subsheaf F ⊂ E of rank less than r, μ(F) ≤ μ(E). If the inequality is strict, E is stable.

    Semistability behaves well under tensor products and duals: if E and F are both semistable, so is E ⊗ F. Every vector bundle has a unique Harder–Narasimhan filtration whose successive quotients are semistable.

    Riemann–Roch

    The Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch formula on a smooth projective surface:

    χ(S, E) = rank(E) · χ(OS) + (c1(E) · (c1(E) − KS)) / 2 − c2(E)

    where KS is the canonical divisor and χ is the Euler characteristic h0 − h1 + h2. Applying this to E = End(F) gives the natural setting in which the discriminant Δ(F) appears.

    Bogomolov's Inequality: the Classical Characteristic-0 Case

    Statement of the Theorem

    Theorem (Bogomolov, 1978): let k be an algebraically closed field of characteristic 0, S a smooth projective surface over k, and H an ample divisor. If E is an H-semistable vector bundle, then

    Δ(E) = 2r · c2(E) − (r−1) · c1(E)2 ≥ 0

    Equality holds if and only if E is a direct sum of line bundles (after an étale cover).

    The Idea of the Proof in Characteristic 0

    Two main approaches: a differential-geometric one (Kobayashi–Hitchin correspondence) and an algebraic one (the Mehta–Ramanathan restriction theorem).

    Algebraic approach, key steps:

    1. Restriction theorem (Mehta–Ramanathan): if E is H-semistable, then for a general H-hyperplane section curve C ⊂ S, the restriction E|C is also semistable.
    2. Positivity of the bundle: for a semistable bundle on a curve, Riemann–Roch and cohomology vanishing show that End(E|C) is "positive" in the algebraic sense.
    3. Lift back to the surface: lift the curve-level conclusion using the Hodge index theorem to get Δ(E) ≥ 0.

    In characteristic 0, the restriction theorem in step 1 has a strong form: stability is preserved on a general section. This relies on the "separability" present in characteristic 0, where Frobenius doesn't intervene.

    The Frobenius Morphism

    Definition and Basic Properties

    Let k be a field of characteristic p > 0 and X a scheme over k. The absolute Frobenius Fabs: X → X is the identity on the underlying topological space and the map f ↦ fp on the structure sheaf.

    Since f ↦ fp is not a k-algebra homomorphism (unless k is perfect), it's more natural to use the relative Frobenius. Let X(p) be the base change of X by the Frobenius Fk: k → k (a ↦ ap). The relative Frobenius F = FX/k: X → X(p) is a k-morphism.

    Key properties:

    • For a line bundle L: F*L = L⊗p, i.e., c1(F*L) = p · c1(L)
    • For a vector bundle E: rank(F*E) = rank(E), and c1(F*E) = p · c1(E)
    • Frobenius pullback does not in general preserve stability: even if E is semistable, F*E can fail to be

    Frobenius Stability (Strong Stability)

    This leads to a stability stratification specific to positive characteristic:

    • Semistable: the standard Mumford–Takemoto definition
    • Frobenius semistable (F-semistable, strongly semistable): E is strongly semistable if (Fn)*E is semistable for every n ≥ 1

    Strong semistability is the stronger condition. Strongly semistable bundles satisfy a clean version of the Bogomolov inequality: if E is strongly semistable, then Δ(E) ≥ 0 (in any characteristic).

    For a generic semistable but not strongly semistable bundle E, the Harder–Narasimhan filtration of (Fn)*E eventually stabilizes, and the resulting "Frobenius instability" can be described via p-curvature or Higgs fields. This is the subject of work by Langer, Mehta–Pauly, and others.

    Quasi-Elliptic Fibrations

    Elliptic and Quasi-Elliptic Fibrations

    Let f: S → C be a morphism from a smooth projective surface to a smooth projective curve with smooth, connected generic fiber. If the generic fiber is a smooth genus-1 curve (an elliptic curve), f is an elliptic fibration.

    A quasi-elliptic fibration is different: the generic fiber is a rational curve with a single cusp, with arithmetic genus 1 but geometric genus 0. Specifically, the generic fiber is isomorphic to the affine curve y2 = x3 (in characteristic 2) or y2 = x3 (in some characteristic-3 cases), plus a "point at infinity."

    Why They Exist Only in Characteristics 2 and 3

    In characteristic 0 or characteristic ≥ 5, quasi-elliptic fibrations don't exist. The reason is separability:

    The normalization map ℙ1 → (cuspidal curve) is separable in characteristic ≠ 2, 3, so a section through a general fiber would force an "embedded smooth curve," contradicting the geometric genus of the fiber.

    In characteristic 2, the map t ↦ (t2, t3) is inseparable (a purely inseparable map of degree 2), which is what allows the cuspidal curve to behave as a "genus-1 object." Characteristic 3 has an analogous construction. The inseparability is exactly the influence of Frobenius.

    Geometric Properties of Quasi-Elliptic Surfaces

    Surfaces carrying a quasi-elliptic fibration (quasi-elliptic surfaces) have these properties:

    • Kodaira dimension: κ(S) = 1 (the "quasi-elliptic" case)
    • Canonical bundle formula: similar to the elliptic case, but with extra terms accounting for the inseparable fibration
    • Singular fibers: special singular types; Kodaira's classification partly applies, partly needs adjustment
    • Frobenius behavior: because the fiber structure is inseparable, Frobenius along the fiber direction acts very differently from the elliptic case

    Quasi-elliptic surfaces are one of the few phenomena in positive-characteristic algebraic geometry that are genuinely different from characteristic 0 — they exist precisely because of the special interplay between separability and inseparability in characteristics 2 and 3.

    Why the Classical Bogomolov Proof Fails Here

    Where the Argument Breaks

    Extending the characteristic-0 Bogomolov proof to positive characteristic typically goes:

    1. If E is strongly semistable, get Δ(E) ≥ 0 directly
    2. If E is not strongly semistable, find an n such that the Harder–Narasimhan filtration of (Fn)*E gives a destabilizing subsheaf, then use the curve version of the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality to get a bound.

    This goes through on a typical surface (for sufficiently large p). On a quasi-elliptic surface, step 2 hits real obstacles:

    Obstacle 1: inseparability of Frobenius along the fiber. For a quasi-elliptic fibration f: S → C, the generic fiber Xη is a cuspidal rational curve, and its normalization ν: ℙ1 → Xη is purely inseparable of degree p. So a vector bundle pushed down from ℙ1, when restricted to the fiber, gets extra destabilization under Frobenius pullback whose magnitude is hard to control.

    Obstacle 2: missing sections. In a standard elliptic fibration, a section σ: C → S provides geometric structure (Mordell–Weil group, etc.). Quasi-elliptic fibrations generally have no section (or only finitely many), which disables techniques that rely on sections.

    Obstacle 3: curvature and p-curvature. In positive characteristic, vector-bundle stability is closely tied to p-curvature (an invariant of the Frobenius flat structure). In the quasi-elliptic case, the behavior of p-curvature along the fiber is determined by the inseparable fibration rather than by the bundle's intrinsic structure. This breaks the usual intuition that "stable ⟺ bounded p-curvature."

    A Concrete Failure Mode

    Let S be a quasi-elliptic K3 surface in characteristic 2, with quasi-elliptic fibration f: S → ℙ1. Consider the rank-2 vector bundle associated to the fiber structure, E = f*(V) ⊗ L, where V is a rank-2 bundle on ℙ1 and L is a suitably chosen line bundle.

    E can be semistable, but F*E becomes unstable because of how Frobenius acts along the inseparable fiber direction. The standard "iterate Frobenius pullback" argument can't give a uniform bound here, and a finer analysis using the quasi-elliptic structure is needed.

    Main Result and Proof Strategy

    Main Theorem

    Theorem (see paper): let k be an algebraically closed field of characteristic 2 or 3, S a quasi-elliptic surface over k, and H an ample divisor. If E is an H-semistable vector bundle, then

    Δ(E) ≥ 0

    i.e., the Bogomolov-type inequality holds on quasi-elliptic surfaces.

    Proof Strategy

    The proof uses the structure of the quasi-elliptic fibration f: S → C, in two steps:

    Step 1: analysis along the fibers. Restrict E to a quasi-elliptic fiber Xt, then use the fine classification of vector bundles on a cuspidal rational curve (together with the inseparability of the normalization ν: ℙ1 → Xt) to analyze the behavior of E|Xt under Frobenius. The Frobenius action on a single fiber is uncontrolled, but the semicontinuity of "the family over C" gives enough constraint.

    Step 2: global argument from the fibration structure. The fiber-level information from step 1 lifts via the Leray spectral sequence and the relative Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch to a global Chern-class identity. The key inequality comes from analyzing f*(End(E)) — the quasi-elliptic structure forces this pushforward bundle on C to carry a special filtration, which supplies the non-negative lower bound on Δ(E).

    The most delicate part is handling the contribution from wild fibers. In the quasi-elliptic case, the Euler-characteristic computation for singular fibers picks up a "wild" term from the inseparable fibration (related to the inseparable degree in positive characteristic), and these contributions have to be separated out and shown not to break Δ ≥ 0.

    Background and Extensions

    Bogomolov–Gieseker Inequality and Moduli Spaces

    A direct consequence of the Bogomolov inequality is the boundedness of the moduli space of vector bundles: rank-r, H-semistable bundles with fixed c1 have c2 bounded below by ((r−1)/(2r)) · c12. This guarantees that the moduli space MS(r, c1, c2) is of finite type for fixed c2, opening the door to Donaldson–Uhlenbeck–Yau type structure.

    Extending this to quasi-elliptic surfaces lays groundwork for studying vector-bundle moduli spaces in positive characteristic.

    Simpson Correspondence in Positive Characteristic

    In characteristic 0, the Kobayashi–Hitchin correspondence sets up an equivalence between stable vector bundles (algebraic geometry) and Hermitian–Einstein connections (differential geometry). In positive characteristic this is replaced by work of Ogus–Vologodsky (the characteristic-p Riemann–Hilbert correspondence), Chen–Zhu, and others. On quasi-elliptic surfaces, the inseparable fibration makes the correspondence harder to construct — this is an open problem.

    Connection with Artin Supersingular Surfaces

    Quasi-elliptic K3 surfaces (the special cases in characteristic 2 and 3) are closely related to Artin supersingular K3 surfaces. The Shioda–Inose construction in characteristic 0 connects K3 surfaces to pairs of elliptic curves; in characteristic 2, the quasi-elliptic structure plays the role of the elliptic-curve pair, reflecting how Frobenius shapes the Picard-group structure. This is an active research direction.
